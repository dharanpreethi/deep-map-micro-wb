About the project

Revisiting Indian Micro-Waterbodies

An interactive deep map of literary, cultural, historical and environmental memories

Deep Mapping of Micro Water Bodies (DMIMW) is among the first Blue Digital Humanities projects from India. It puts the country's small ponds, tanks, kunds, canals and channels on one open map and reads each of them through four layers of memory.

Project brief

Small waters, deep memories

The idea grew out of the AHRC-funded project Digital Innovation in Water Scarcity in Coimbatore, India, where Shanmugapriya T. worked as a postdoctoral research associate. That work showed how little attention colonial and postcolonial governments paid to micro water bodies: the channels, canals, weirs, streams, ponds, rivulets and tanks that gather water across hills and plains and feed the big dams and lakes.

These waters are local. Each one belongs to a district or a region and matters to the people who live beside it, keeping water, culture and community close together. We ask how they become part of historical, cultural, literary and ecological memory, and we answer by writing a thick description of each one.

The map holds water bodies from several Indian states, and the team keeps adding more.

Small waters remain the least investigated part of the water environment.Biggs, von Fumetti & Kelly-Quinn, Hydrobiologia (2017)

Method and outcome

Deep mapping through Interactive Digital Narrative

Deep mapping

Decides what to gather

Most digital humanities methods count things. Deep mapping layers them. The term comes from William Least Heat-Moon's PrairyErth, which borrowed Clifford Geertz's idea of thick description to read a single Kansas county through its geography, history, ecology, memory and stories. David Bodenhamer sees the deep map as a medium that welcomes many voices, even when they disagree.

+
Interactive Digital Narrative

Decides how to show it

Interactive Digital Narrative (IDN), as Hartmut Koenitz describes it, gives those layers a structure you can move through. It is a multimodal system of text, image, sound and map, and your choices decide what you meet next.

The outcome

Put together, the two let you build your own picture of a water body instead of following one fixed story. You choose the state, the water body and the layer to open first. Begin somewhere else and the same place shows you a different arrangement of memory, which is how an overlooked pond or canal starts to mean something.

03
Fish as narrators

Four fish, four ways of remembering

Each layer has its own narrator, and each narrator is an Indian fish. An old golden mahseer tells the history. A rohu, dressed like the fish at a Bengali wedding, tells the culture. A bookish hilsa looks after the literature, and a distressed magur catfish speaks for the environment. The fish make the four layers easy to tell apart. They also remind us of what the map still cannot hear: the memories water shares with other species.

Built with open tools

From native regions to an open map

Collect where we belong

Each team member gathered material from their home region or one nearby: West Bengal, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Knowing the places and their context made them easier to read.

Read the open traces

Our sources are Wikipedia, travel blogs, community websites, social media, news reports and research papers. Every layer has a short written account with links, so you can check the sources yourself.

Add image and sound

Photographs, plus links to videos, documentaries and posts, show you what each place looks like. The soundscapes, in the style of the tanpura, bansuri, santoor, ektara and temple bells, are generated in code, so there are no audio files to download.

Build with open tools

Aditya Kumar and Simanta Nandi designed and built the site, with input from Shanmugapriya T. and Simran Bhimjyani. It runs on open web technology and open map data, and GitHub hosts it free of charge, so anyone can use it without a login or a fee.

The technology, in brief

HTML, CSS and JavaScript
Plain web code. Nothing to install and no app to download.
MapLibre GL JS
An open-source library that draws the 3D globe, the relief and the markers.
OpenStreetMap and CARTO
Open map data, restyled here as the Pothi and Raat base maps.
AWS Terrain Tiles
Open elevation data that gives the hills their height.
PapaParse
Reads our table of water bodies directly in the browser.
Web Audio API
Plays the six soundscapes live, with no recordings.
Google Translate
Opens each page in eight Indian languages.
GitHub Pages
Free hosting, straight from the project's open repository.
