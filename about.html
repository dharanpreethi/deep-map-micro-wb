About the project

Revisiting Indian Micro-Waterbodies

An interactive deep map of literary, cultural, historical and environmental memories

Deep Mapping of Micro Water Bodies (DMIMW) is among the first Blue Digital Humanities projects from India. It documents the overlooked ponds, tanks, kunds, canals and channels of the subcontinent through four layers of memory, on a map anyone can explore.

Project brief

Small waters, deep memories

The idea grew out of the AHRC-funded project Digital Innovation in Water Scarcity in Coimbatore, India, where Shanmugapriya T. worked as a postdoctoral research associate. That work showed that micro water bodies — channels, canals, weirs, streams, ponds, rivulets and tanks — received little attention from colonial or postcolonial governments, even though they are what gather water across mountains and feed the large dams and lakes.

These waters are local, situated within a district or region, and culturally significant to the people who live beside them. They reduce the distance between water, culture and people and help build collective consciousness. This project studies how they become part of cultural, historical, literary and ecological memory by creating thick descriptions of each one.

The platform currently maps water bodies across several Indian states, and grows as the team adds more.

Small waters remain the least investigated part of the water environment.Biggs, von Fumetti & Kelly-Quinn, Hydrobiologia (2017)
Research questions

Three questions guide the work

  1. How can deep mapping reveal the cultural, historical, literary and environmental significance of micro water bodies in India?
  2. How is the memory of micro water bodies preserved in historical records, imagined in literary texts, practised in cultural traditions and sustained in ecological systems?
  3. How can digital humanities tools help document the memories of micro water bodies?
Objectives

What the project sets out to do

Make the overlooked visible

Place India's micro water bodies on an interactive map, beside the dominant narratives of large rivers and dams.

Layer memory, not flatten it

Record each site through historical, cultural, literary and environmental layers that can be read in any order.

Speak to everyone

Serve Blue Humanities scholars and the general public alike, with plain language, translation and accessible design.

Raise ecological urgency

Show where sacred and literary memory sits beside pollution, siltation and neglect, and press for care.

Stay open

Build with open tools and open data, host for free, and keep the platform open access beyond the academy.

Grow a water lexicon

Collect the many names for water bodies in the team's languages towards a multilingual dictionary.

Method

Deep mapping through Interactive Digital Narrative

Most digital humanities methods favour quantification. Deep mapping favours layering, multiplicity and plurality. The term comes from William Least Heat-Moon's PrairyErth, which borrowed Clifford Geertz's idea of thick description to explore a single Kansas county through geography, history, ecology, memory and story. David Bodenhamer describes the deep map as a medium that encourages multiplicity and competing perspectives.

Interactive Digital Narrative (IDN), as theorised by Hartmut Koenitz, gives that layering an architecture: a multimodal system in which the reader's choices shape what they encounter. If deep mapping decides what to gather, IDN decides how to present it. Together they let you build your own understanding of a water body instead of following a single story.

Four properties

Procedural & participatory
Clicks, layers and filters respond to every choice you make.
Spatial
The map of India is the main way through the archive — from globe to state to site.
Encyclopaedic
Dozens of water bodies across several states, each with four layers and linked sources.

Four aesthetic qualities

Immersion
The 3D map, photographs, soundscapes and the fish narrators hold your attention.
Agency
You choose the state, the water body and the layer to enter first.
Transformation & kaleidoscope
The same place yields a different arrangement of memory depending on where you begin — overlooked becomes meaningful.
Four layers & the fish narrators

Four fish, four ways of remembering

Four Indian fish narrate the four layers: an aged golden mahseer for history, a rohu dressed like a Bengali wedding fish for culture, a scholarly hilsa for literature and a distressed magur catfish for the environment. They make the layering legible for a general audience, and remind us of what remains unheard: the memories water shares with other species.

How we built it

From native regions to an open map

Collect where we belong

Each team member gathered data from their native or nearby regions — West Bengal, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu — for familiarity with the sites and their contexts.

Read the open traces

Sources include Wikipedia, travel blogs, community websites, social media, news reports and research papers. Each layer gets a brief written account, with links so readers can follow the sources themselves.

Add image and sound

Photographs, links to videos, documentaries and posts animate each place. Soundscapes in the style of tanpura, bansuri, santoor, ektara and temple bells are generated in code, so no audio files need to download.

Build with open tools

The site was designed and built by Aditya Kumar and Simanta Nandi with inputs from Shanmugapriya T. and Simran Bhimjyani, using HTML, CSS and JavaScript, OpenStreetMap and CARTO, and hosted for free on GitHub.

Findings

What the deep map makes visible

Memory and protection run on separate axes. Long, diachronic memory survives in ritual, classical literature, mythology and community song. The environmental layer shows the present: the significance of the former does not translate into institutional protection of the latter. The deep map shows that disconnection within a single frame.

Micro water bodies are remembered informally. Large waters are documented by institutions and colonial records. Micro water bodies live in oral tradition, regional literature and mythology, and reach the digital world through Wikipedia, community websites, social media and blogs — sources whose survival depends on platforms and on the community's continued interest.

The naming itself is a form of memory. Damodar and Adi Ganga carry mythological connections; the Kalingarayan Canal carries the name of the chieftain who built it, and with it a story of social obligation and collective identity.

Related Indian projects

Prototype Blue DH work from India

Several Indian projects place water, displacement and community memory at the centre of digital practice without calling themselves Blue DH. None uses deep mapping through IDN as its main method — the gap this project addresses.

Lost Water! Remainscape?

Shanmugapriya T.'s eco-electronic literature work on the lost waterscapes of the Noyyal River, Coimbatore, across three generations, using interactivity, kinetic typography and archival maps.

Oral History Narmada

Nandini Oza's archive of testimony from the movement against the Sardar Sarovar dam, built from over twenty years of fieldwork.

Jol Jyanto Kolkata

An immersive exhibition by the Living Waters Museum mapping Kolkata's ponds, dighis, canals, the Adi Ganga and the East Kolkata Wetlands.

The Southern Collective

A collaboratory for the Northern Indian Ocean, with the Migration Diaries and the Sea Lexicon of coastal knowledge.

Limitations & next steps

An unfinished map, on purpose

The four layers are not equally available for every water body, and the project currently focuses on the human–water relationship while overlooking water's connections with other species — an absence the fish narrator gestures towards. The project also relies on the uneven availability of open digital sources.

Next, we will deepen the layers, add water bodies from existing and new regions, grow the lexicon of water names gathered in the team's four languages into a multilingual dictionary, and study literary and non-literary texts with computational methods.
