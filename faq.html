Fifteen questions visitors often ask about the project, the deep map and how to use it.

01The projectWhat is the Deep Mapping of Micro Water Bodies project?

It is an interactive deep map of India's small, often overlooked water bodies — ponds, kunds, tanks, canals, channels and lakes. Each one is documented through four layers of memory: historical, cultural, literary and environmental. The project is based in the Department of Humanities and Social Sciences at IIT (ISM) Dhanbad.

02The projectWhat counts as a micro water body?

We use the term for small, local water bodies situated within a district or region and culturally significant to the people around them: channels, canals, check dams, weirs, rivulets, ponds and tanks. They gather water across the landscape and feed the large rivers, dams and lakes that usually receive all the attention.

03MethodWhat is deep mapping?

Deep mapping is a layered way of describing a place. The term comes from William Least Heat-Moon's PrairyErth, which borrowed the anthropologist Clifford Geertz's idea of thick description. Instead of plotting a single point, a deep map gathers history, memory, story, ritual and ecology so that many perspectives sit together.

04MethodWhat is Blue Digital Humanities?

Blue Humanities studies water — oceans, rivers, wetlands — as a cultural and historical presence. Blue Digital Humanities does this with digital tools. This project is among the first Blue DH projects from India and one of the few to focus on micro water bodies rather than oceans and rivers.

05MethodWhat are the four layers of memory?

Historical: the water body's origins and past significance. Cultural: how it is used today for ritual, religion and community life. Literary: how it appears in fiction, poetry, travel writing and local storytelling. Environmental: the pollution, siltation and neglect it now faces.

06MethodWho is the fish narrator?

Four Indian fish narrate the four layers. An aged golden mahseer with white whiskers and spectacles tells the history; a rohu dressed like a Bengali wedding fish, with turmeric, alpana, a marigold garland and a nath, tells the culture; a scholarly hilsa with a quill and palm-leaf pothi tells the literature; and a distressed magur catfish in murky water tells the environmental story. Together they remind us of the memories water shares with other species, which the project has yet to record.

07Using the siteHow do I use the 3D map?

Open the 3D map, then click a kalash pin or a raised state to fly into it. Click a water-body medallion to open its folio, then choose one of the four fish narrators to open its layer. Drag to move, scroll to zoom, and right-drag or Ctrl + drag to tilt and rotate. Jal Yatra lets you browse everything as cards.

08Using the siteWhy do some layers say “being researched”?

Digital information about micro water bodies is scarce and uneven. Where a layer has not been written yet, the folio says so and lists any sources gathered so far. The ring around each map marker shows which layers are complete, which have sources only and which are still pending.

09Using the siteCan I read the site in Hindi, Bengali or other Indian languages?

Yes. Use the language button (文A) at the top of any page and choose Hindi, Bengali, Gujarati, Odia, Tamil, Bhojpuri, Maithili or Santali. The page reopens translated by Google Translate, and the links you follow stay in that language; choose English to return to the original. This is machine translation, so names and scripts of water bodies stay in their original form. Translation works on the published website only, because Google can translate public pages only.

10Using the siteWhat accessibility options are available?

The accessibility button in the header lets you enlarge text up to 140%, switch between light, dark and automatic appearance, turn on high contrast, reduce motion, use a highly readable font and underline every link. Everything works with the keyboard, and your choices are remembered on your device.

11Using the siteWhere does the music come from?

The site plays a theme and five water-body soundscapes — lake, pond, kund, canal and river — generated live in your browser in the style of the tanpura, bansuri, santoor, ektara and temple bells. No audio files are downloaded. Browsers start sound only after your first tap or click, and the speaker button turns it off at any time. See the sound credits.

12Data & sourcesWhere does the data come from?

Each team member collected data from their native or nearby regions. Sources are openly available: Wikipedia, travel blogs, community websites, social media, news reports and research papers. Every layer links to the sources it draws on so you can read further.

13Data & sourcesCan I suggest a water body or correct an entry?

Please do. Write to us through the contact page with the name and location of the water body, what you know about it, and any sources or photographs you can share with permission. Corrections, credits and removal requests are always welcome.

14ReuseCan I reuse the content, and how do I cite it?

The project's text and data are licensed under CC BY-NC 4.0: you may share and adapt them for non-commercial purposes with credit. The citation page gives ready-made references in MLA, APA, Chicago, Harvard, IEEE, BibTeX and RIS, including citations for a single water body.

15The projectWho made the project, and how is it funded?

The project is made by Shanmugapriya T., Simran Bhimjyani, Simanta Nandi and Aditya Kumar in the Department of Humanities and Social Sciences, IIT (ISM) Dhanbad. It is a non-commercial academic initiative built with open tools and hosted for free on GitHub Pages. Meet the team.
